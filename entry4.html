Lost and found: natural selection in ancient European genomes

Weeks 9–11: selection; molecular evolution; quantitative genetics

Article: "Scientists use ancient DNA to shed light on adaptation of early Europeans" (2024), ScienceDaily  ·  Original paper: Pandey et al. (2024), Nature Communications

Modern genomes preserve only eroded traces of past selection: drift, recombination and admixture blur the footprints of adaptation. Ancient DNA offers what modern data cannot — a time series in which the same population is sampled before and after a selective event. Can such a series recover what drift has erased?

The study

Pandey et al. (2024) assembled 708 ancient European genomes spanning about 7,000 years and grouped them into four periods. Instead of allele-frequency tests, they applied G12, a multi-locus statistic that detects haplotypes raised to high frequency by selection and works on pseudo-haploid data — the only format low-coverage ancient DNA allows. In simulations and at validated loci (LCT, TLR1, SLC24A5), G12 outperformed SweepFinder2. Across the time transect it identified 14 candidate regions; 11 appeared in only one period or its neighbour, HLA was shared by all four, and LCT was significant only in the Historical period.

Reflect & Connect

This connects to the selection content of Weeks 9–11. Directional selection should drive a favourable allele towards fixation, yet here most signals disappear between periods, as Figure 1 shows. The DDT-resistance example showed that selection can reverse when the environment changes; this paper shows that even without reversal the signature can be overwritten. Neutral theory makes the same point from the other side: most molecular change is drift, so a true sweep must be strong enough to stand out. The immune signals also echo the SARS-CoV-2 variant tracking from class.

Strengths & Limitations

Strengths include the sample size, the simulation benchmarking and the functional positive controls. But pseudo-haploid genotypes discard heterozygosity, the scan detects mainly strong selection, windows cannot pinpoint the causal allele, and every sample is European. The authors also cannot distinguish drift from admixture as the cause of the missing signals.

Insights & Curiosity

I looked up rs4988235 in my own genotype data: an allele discussed in Week 8 sits in my own file. What I want to understand next is how a selection signal can be localised to a single variant, and whether this approach transfers to populations outside Europe, where most ancient-DNA time transects do not yet exist.
