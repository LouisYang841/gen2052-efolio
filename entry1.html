De-extinction: resurrection or high-tech replacement?

Weeks 1–3: DNA cloning and genomics; genome content — protein-coding genes

Article: Schnebly & Minteer (2023), The Conversation  ·  Original paper: Sherkow & Greely (2013), Science

Nowadays, "resurrecting" mammoths repeatedly appears in commercial advertising and social media. But from a genetics perspective, it's much more complicated: are we resurrecting a species, or creating new species?

Schnebly and Minteer (2023) point out that the de-extinction narrative is a misleading "resurrection" story. Sherkow and Greely (2013) summarized three primary methods: back-breeding, somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT), and genome editing.

A Close Approximation

De-extinction research is not without value: it pushes gene-editing technologies further and provides rescue tools for nearly extinct species. But the creature it creates can only be an approximation.

Without the original cytoplasmic environment of the egg, full epigenetic marks, symbiotic microbes, and companions from its own species, a "resurrected" mammoth can never return to and support the original ecological niche of the mammoth in history.

Reflect & Connect

The three technological approaches of de-extinction mentioned earlier are directly related to the first three weeks in GEN2052. In relation to genetic sequencing (Week 2): ancient DNA is highly fragmented, even with the third generation long-read sequencing, we still have to face the low quality level and repeated sequences being hard to assemble.

At the genome level (Week 3): as shown in Figure 1, protein-coding sequences make up only 1–2% of the genome — so even if the encoding sequences of Asian elephants were replaced with mammoth versions using multiplex CRISPR (Week 1), the non-coding regulatory regions would still be impossible to recover. Knowing this, I now have a better understanding of the technical boundaries of current genetic engineering technology.

Intrigue & Optimism

The article gives me new insights about the relationship between genome and living organism: the genome is not simply a code which can directly replicate an organism from it. Using terms from computer science, the genome is stateless, while an organism is stateful and cannot be easily reproduced from a genome. Moving forward, I want to explore comparing genomes to computer operating systems in terms of the topology and evolution of their regulatory control networks.
