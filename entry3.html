Population bottlenecks and the peopling of Papua New Guinea

Weeks 7–8: introduction to population and quantitative genetics; Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium and genetic drift

Article: "Who are the Papua New Guineans?" (2025), ScienceDaily  ·  Original paper: Mondal et al. (2025), Nature Communications

Papua New Guinea (PNG) was settled early, and its people carry about 3% Denisovan ancestry. Relative cross-coalescent rate (RCCR) curves had suggested that PNG genomes separated from African genomes around 100,000 years ago, earlier than other Out-of-Africa populations, which was read as evidence for ancestry from an earlier wave out of Africa. Mondal et al. (2025) re-tested that claim.

The study

Using newly published genomes and a neural network trained on simulated frequency spectra, the authors compared five demographic models. The best fit places PNG as a sister group to East Asians, with no substantial early out-of-Africa contribution. The apparent early separation — the shift in Figure 1b, where the PNG–African curve sits apart from the other pairs — is an artefact of PNG's own history: a severe bottleneck cut its effective population size to about 670, compared with roughly 3,500 in Europeans, and growth afterwards was slower. A ghost contribution below 5% cannot be excluded, but it is no longer needed to explain the curves in Figure 1b.

Reflect & Connect

The result is a lesson in genetic drift. Allele frequencies change by chance every generation, and the strength of that change scales with 1/N: in a small population a neutral allele's chance of fixation is 1/(2N). A bottleneck is drift at its most extreme — it does not just shrink a population, it distorts its history. Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium assumes an infinitely large population, and PNG violates this assumption, so the allele-frequency calculations we practised in workshops cannot describe it.

Strengths & Limitations

The study's strength is triangulation: three independent frequency-spectrum datasets, simulated models, and an honest admission that contributions below 5% would be misclassified. The limitation is that conclusions are conditional on the models tested, and the tropical ancient DNA that could test them directly is scarce.

Insights & Curiosity

On GEDmatch my own data returns only a rough genetic distance; this paper turns the same question into a whole-genome time series read through a neural network. I now see drift as a force that can imitate history, and I want to know what independent evidence could separate a ghost population from a bottleneck.
